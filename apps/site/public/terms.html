Keepsake is small and free on purpose. These terms are short on purpose too — here's the deal, in plain language.

Where Keepsake is available

Keepsake currently serves the United States and Canada. It isn't available in the United Kingdom right now — not because of anything about you, but because the UK's Online Safety Act asks every service like this one for a written safety assessment, a children's-access review, and a formal complaints process, and we haven't taken that work on yet for a free project run by one person. This may change; we'd rather say so plainly than pretend it isn't a choice.

Minimum age

You must be 18 or older to give or request anything on Keepsake, and we ask you to confirm that each time you post a listing or ask for one. We can't verify anyone's age — this is a self-declared limit, not an age-verification system. We set it higher than a typical content site's 13-or-so because Keepsake's whole premise is arranging in-person meetings between strangers, which is a different, higher-stakes thing than reading or posting content. If we learn — through a report, a listing, or a request — that someone using Keepsake is under 18, we'll remove the listing or request involved and, if it's a giver, ban their device token.

Gifting only, never sales

Keepsake exists to help people pass sentimental objects on to someone who'll love them. You may not use it to sell, auction, trade, or barter anything. If money or goods change hands off-platform in exchange for a listing, that's a use of Keepsake we don't allow.

You have to own it

Only post something you own outright and have the right to give away. Don't post on behalf of someone else, and don't post anything you found, borrowed, or don't actually have in hand.

What's not allowed

  • Anything illegal to give away where you live.
  • Weapons, drugs, hazardous materials, live animals, or anything requiring a license to transfer.
  • Contact details — phone numbers, emails, social handles, addresses — inside a story or title. Keep contact exchange inside Keepsake's request flow, where it belongs.
  • Anything sexually explicit, hateful, or intended to harass.

Specific items — what's actually illegal versus what's just a bad idea

The table below spells out some categories in more detail, because we think a rule you can't check for yourself isn't much of a rule. The "Basis" column matters: where a row rests on an actual statute, we say so. Where it doesn't — where we're banning something because it's genuinely hazardous and unverifiable, not because a law says so — we call that a hazard-only rule instead of calling it illegal. A free bike helmet isn't illegal to give away, for instance; we still ask you not to, because we have no way to check its impact history, but we're not going to lie to you about why.

Item Rule Basis
Cribs made before June 28, 2011, and all drop-side cribs Do not give away Statute-adjacent — the CPSC's own written guidance says these "should not [be] resold, donate[d] or g[iven] away," interpreting the federal crib safety rules
Inclined sleepers and crib bumpers Banned — do not give away, regardless of age Statute (federal law, Pub. L. 117-126)
Used children's products with lead above federal limits (except metal jewelry) Generally fine once used, unless you actually know it violates the limit Statute (15 U.S.C. §1278a(b)(7) exempts used children's products from lead limits even when donated)
Children's metal jewelry (used) Do not give away without verifying lead content Statute — specifically carved out of the used-goods exemption above
Car seats Allowed — disclose age and any crash history in your listing Hazard-only. Not federally restricted once first sold; we ask for disclosure because we can't verify crash or recall history ourselves
Helmets (bike, sports) Allowed — disclose age and any impact history in your listing Hazard-only. No law restricts giving these away; we ask for disclosure for the same reason as car seats
Radioactive antiques (vaseline glass, some Fiestaware) Allowed — note "not for food or drink contact" Statute — expressly exempt under federal regulation (10 CFR §§30.15/40.13)
Weapons, ammunition, explosives Do not give away Statute, varying by item and jurisdiction — several categories require a licensed transfer wherever you are
Drugs, controlled substances Do not give away Statute (federal and state controlled-substances law)
Live animals Do not give away through Keepsake Hazard-only as a platform-fit decision (we can't verify care, transport, or welfare conditions); many places also have their own statutes covering animal transfers specifically
Recalled products generally, beyond the categories above Do not give away if you actually know about the recall Hazard-only as a general platform rule, modeled on the "actual knowledge" standard federal law uses for the specific categories above — but not itself a universal statute

One row above deserves an extra word of honesty: the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's own published guidance says its rules reach "individuals holding yard sales" and that people who give away used products for free "cannot sell or donate products that do not meet the requirements of the law." No court has actually decided whether that reach extends to a free, one-to-one platform like this one — it's the regulator's stated position, not a settled legal fact, and we're telling you that distinction rather than dressing up an untested claim as certain.

We may remove listings

Every listing is screened before it goes live, and anyone can report a listing that's already up. We may remove a listing, decline to publish it, or ban a device token, at our discretion, with or without explanation.

Meeting up safely

A keepsake means a stranger comes to a door or a meeting spot for something that often means more to the giver than an ordinary object would — which makes the ordinary rules for meeting someone from the internet worth repeating here, not just assuming you know them:

  • Meet somewhere public and well-lit — a coffee shop, a store entrance, a police department's designated exchange spot if your town has one. Not your home, and not theirs, especially the first time.
  • Bring a friend, or tell someone else the time and place before you go.
  • Keep the conversation inside Keepsake's request messages for as long as you can, instead of moving straight to text or a phone call — it's a smaller thing to hand a stranger than your number, and it keeps a record either of you can point back to.
  • No-contact counts as meeting up. If either of you would rather leave the keepsake on a porch, with a neighbor, or at a front desk than meet face to face, say so in your note or your reply — that's a normal way to use Keepsake, not an awkward one.
  • If anything about a planned meetup feels off, you can back out. That instinct is worth more than a sense of obligation to a stranger you agreed to meet.

This is guidance, not a guarantee, and it isn't a substitute for your own judgment about a specific person or place — see "No warranty" below for what Keepsake is and isn't responsible for once you're arranging a handoff.

No warranty

Keepsake connects people; it doesn't inspect, authenticate, or guarantee anything about the items listed. Keepsakes are given "as is." We're not responsible for the condition of an item, whether a handoff happens smoothly, or what happens after you meet up. Use good judgment when arranging to meet a stranger — see "Meeting up safely" above for what that looks like in practice.

Your account, such as it is

You don't have a password-protected account — you have a private device key. You're responsible for keeping it safe, and we're not able to prove a listing is yours if you lose it without a recovery email on file.
