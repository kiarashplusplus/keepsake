# Story, Provenance, and Desirability: Research Findings for Keepsake

## Verdict

The evidence strongly supports that a story raises desirability of a secondhand object — but the *valence of the story's origin* determines the direction and size of the effect, and this is not merely correlational: **Newman, Diesendruck & Bloom (2011, "Celebrity Contagion and the Value of Objects," *Journal of Consumer Research* 38(2):215–228, DOI 10.1086/658999)** is the single strongest source, showing experimentally that increased "contagion" (more physical/personal contact with the prior owner) *raises* purchase intent for objects tied to positively-regarded people and *lowers* it for objects tied to despised people — the same mechanism cutting both ways. The caveat: their negative-figure stimuli were extreme (murderers, despised public figures), not mundane "my ex" or "my divorce" provenance, so the magnitude of the penalty for an ordinary breakup story is unverified and likely much smaller — possibly even net-neutral or positive if the story signals resolution/growth rather than raw loss.

## Findings

- **Significant Objects project** (Rob Walker & Joshua Glenn, 2009–2010) — thrift objects bought at ~$1.25 avg., $128.74 total, sold on eBay with invented short stories for **$3,612.51 total** (~2,700% markup). [robwalker.net/significantobjects](https://robwalker.net/significantobjects/), [significantobjects.com](https://significantobjects.com/about/) — [search snippet only, consistent across multiple sources]. Critique: listings disclosed the author byline, so buyers knew stories were fictional — the premium is a "narrative-presence" effect, not a "true story" effect, and there's no controlled non-story comparison group, so eBay/novelty confounds aren't ruled out — [my synthesis, not independently sourced].
- **Newman, Diesendruck & Bloom 2011** — contagion (belief that owner's "essence" transfers) is the driver of celebrity-object premiums; effect **reverses sign for disliked/despised owners**, where more contact lowers purchase intent. [academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/38/2/215](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/38/2/215/1893185); summary via [AcaWiki](https://acawiki.org/Celebrity_Contagion_and_the_Value_of_Objects) — [verified by fetch of secondary summary].
- **Rozin/Nemeroff "Hitler's sweater"** — people refuse to wear a sweater once owned by Hitler even after washing, tearing, or having it "cleansed" by a saint; negative moral contagion is non-rational and resistant to physical cleaning, unlike positive contagion. [bigthink.com](https://bigthink.com/guest-thinkers/a-real-life-version-of-the-hitlers-sweater-experiment/) — [search snippet only]. Rozin & Royzman's negativity-bias/negativity-dominance work confirms negative contagion is stronger and stickier than positive contagion generally. [journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0504_2](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0504_2) — [search snippet only].
- **Hood & Bloom, "Art and Authenticity"** and related work — adults and even young children value a genuine original over an identical duplicate, attributing invisible "essence" to authentic objects/attachment items. [minddevlab.yale.edu/.../art-and-authenticity.pdf](https://minddevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/art-and-authenticity.pdf) — [search snippet only]. This implies a *true* story (as Keepsake would use, vs. Significant Objects' fabricated ones) should carry at least as much premium.
- **Argo, Dahl & Morales 2006, "Consumer Contamination"** (*Journal of Marketing* 70(2):81–94) — consumers devalue products they believe were touched/used by unknown others, driven by disgust; salience of prior contact matters more than the contact itself. [journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkg.70.2.081](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkg.70.2.081) — [search snippet only]. Relevant baseline: *any* secondhand object carries some contamination discount that a positive story must overcome.
- **Stigmatized property (real estate analogue)** — murder/haunted-house discounts range widely by study: ~2.9% (2001 study), 5.6% (2013 academic study), up to 15–25% for two to three years post-event per appraiser Randall Bell, decaying over 10–25 years. [thehill.com](https://thehill.com/homenews/3699660-murder-houses-how-does-violence-impact-sale-prices/), [thehustle.co](https://thehustle.co/what-happens-when-someone-is-murdered-in-a-house) — [search snippet only]. Wide variance suggests publicity/severity, not mere "negative history," drives discount size.
- **Provenance premiums, positive case**: Sotheby's 1996 Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis estate sale totaled **$34M+**; JFK's golf clubs sold near $1M, faux pearls fetched vastly above material value. [jamiesharpinteriors.com](https://jamiesharpinteriors.com/blogs/past-perfect/inside-the-auction-jackie-o) — [search snippet only]. Marilyn Monroe items: "Happy Birthday" dress $4.81M (2016), subway dress $5.6M (2011), a broken pearl strand $13,125 vs. $600–800 estimate. [forbes.com](https://www.forbes.com/sites/maryroeloffs/2024/03/29/marilyn-monroe-dress-sells-at-auction-for-325000--eight-times-its-estimate/) — [search snippet only]. Strong support for positive-provenance premiums; no equivalent negative-provenance auction data found.
- **Secondhand narrative research**: Xu, Li et al., "Narrative Information on Secondhand Products in E-Commerce" (*Marketing Letters* 33(4), 2022) — narrative descriptions increase perceived persuasiveness **only when the product was for the seller's own prior use**; for resale/non-self-use products, factual framing outperforms narrative (mediated by reactance). [researchgate.net/publication/361507525](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361507525_Narrative_information_on_secondhand_products_in_e-commerce) — [search snippet only]. Directly relevant: Keepsake's stories are inherently "self-use, personal history" framing, the condition under which this study says narrative *helps*.
- **Divorce ring platforms** (I Do Now I Don't, Worthy) — both were founded explicitly on breakup/divorce backstory (used in press/marketing to attract sellers), but buyer-facing listings emphasize gemological grading/certification, not the personal story. [nbcnews.com](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna51557920), [worthy.com/how-it-works](https://www.worthy.com/how-it-works/) — [search snippet only]. No data found on price effect of disclosing "this ring is from a divorce" to buyers; resale value drops cited (e.g., $10K ring to $2,500 after 11 years) are attributed to diamond depreciation/lab-grown competition, not stigma specifically. **Not found**: a controlled study isolating divorce-narrative disclosure's price effect.
- **Gift-context evidence**: Buy Nothing Project explicitly states "stories about gifts are hugely popular and help build community" for free giveaways. [buynothingproject.org](https://buynothingproject.org/) — [search snippet only, official site]. Freecycle research (Aptekar, *Social Problems* 2016) documents norms (gratitude, no-show etiquette) but no direct story/desirability data. [academic.oup.com/socpro/article-abstract/63/2/266](https://academic.oup.com/socpro/article-abstract/63/2/266/2461525) — [search snippet only].
- **Museum of Broken Relationships / "haunted item" folk beliefs**: anecdotal/folk-belief evidence only that some buyers actively avoid objects with negative personal history ("bad energy," "haunted"); consistent with Nemeroff/Rozin contagion theory but no peer-reviewed secondhand-market study found. [okwhatever.org](https://www.okwhatever.org/topics/wtf/buying-haunted-thrift-store-items) — [search snippet only, low confidence].

## Risks/Objections

- Newman et al.'s "despised figure" stimuli (murderers, hated celebrities) are far more extreme than an ordinary ex or divorce — extrapolating the sign-reversal to Keepsake's use case is a real inferential leap.
- Significant Objects' fabricated-story design and lack of a no-story control group weaken it as proof that *true* stories help; it mainly proves narrative-framing itself has value, independent of truth.
- The Marketing Letters (2022) finding that narrative hurts persuasiveness for non-self-use resale is a genuine counter-signal, though Keepsake's model (personal-history gifting) sits on the favorable side of that study's own boundary condition.
- No study directly tests "divorce" or "breakup" as a labeled provenance category against a neutral control — this remains the biggest unresolved gap; existing divorce-ring platforms sidestep it by hiding the story rather than testing it.

## Implications for Keepsake's story field / "ex"/divorce shelf

1. Frame negative-relationship stories around resolution, generosity, or moving-on ("I'm ready to let this go so it can matter to someone else") rather than raw loss or animosity — this keeps the narrative in "positive contagion" territory (giver's grace) instead of "despised-source contagion" territory that Newman et al. show suppresses desire.
2. Do not create a labeled "divorce"/"ex" shelf as a browsable category — real-world platforms (I Do Now I Don't, Worthy) use breakup narratives for press/founder-story purposes but strip them from buyer-facing listings, suggesting the market itself treats explicit negative-provenance labeling as a liability, not a draw.
3. Since Keepsake is gift-framed (free, not resale), lean on the Buy Nothing Project's pattern — sentimental stories drive engagement in *gift* contexts even without evidence of price/status motives — but keep story fields optional and requester-initiated (a note written *to get* the object) so the narrative reads as personal-history disclosure rather than a marketed contamination claim.
