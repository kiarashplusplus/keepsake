# The Courtship Angle for Keepsake — Research Brief

**Verdict:** This angle is moderately strong and defensible, but it's an *analogy*, not a proven causal mechanism — no study tests "choosing who gets your keepsake" directly. The single best piece of evidence is the convergent finding across Givi & Galak (2017), Zhang & Epley (2012), and Kumar & Epley (2018/2022): givers *systematically underestimate* how much a sentimental gift, or even a small unprompted kindness, will be valued and appreciated by the recipient — which is exactly the anxiety Keepsake's "who gets my grandmother's teacup" moment is designed to resolve by letting the giver read real, specific notes before choosing. Pair that with real product data (Hinge Roses converting far above plain likes, Bumble's low-effort-message friction raising chat quality) showing that *effortful, personalized signals* outperform generic ones, and you have a genuine behavioral-science case that "write me a note about why you'd love this" is a good mechanic — even though no one has published a study on Keepsake's exact loop.

## Findings

**Sentimental value & gift-giving asymmetries**
- Givi, J. & Galak, J. (2017), *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 27(4), 473–479. "Sentimental value and gift giving: Givers' fears of getting it wrong prevents them from getting it right." Givers choose preference-matching (superficial) gifts over sentimentally valuable ones far more than recipients would want, because givers feel certain a matched gift will land but uncertain a sentimental one will — sentimental gifts are undervalued out of giver anxiety, not recipient indifference. [search snippet only, https://myscp.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.jcps.2017.06.002 — direct fetch blocked (403), sourced via Semantic Scholar/ScienceDirect summaries]
- Flynn, F.J. & Adams, G.S. (2009), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 45(2), 404–409. "Money can't buy love: Asymmetric beliefs about gift price and feelings of appreciation." Givers believe price predicts appreciation; recipients report no such link — thoughtfulness, not cost, is what actually registers. [search snippet only, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103108002175]
- Zhang, Y. & Epley, N. (2012), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 141(4), 667–681. "Exaggerated, mispredicted, and misplaced: When 'it's the thought that counts' in gift exchanges." Thought/effort behind a gift increases the receiver's felt connection to the giver, but only when the receiver is prompted to consider it (e.g., after a "bad" gift) — thoughtfulness has to be made legible to register. [search snippet only, abstract corroborated across ResearchGate/PubMed/ERIC]
- Kumar, A. & Epley, N. (2018), *Psychological Science*; and Kumar/Epley (2022), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* ("A little good goes an unexpectedly long way"). Across scenario and field studies (hot chocolate in a park, lab gift-giving), givers of small unprompted kindnesses to strangers consistently underestimate how positively recipients feel and how grateful they are — an egocentric bias insensitive to the warmth the act actually conveys. [search snippet only, https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fxge0001271, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797618772506]
- Yang & Urminsky, "The foresight of givers" line of work (SSRN "Smile-Seeking Givers and Value-Seeking Recipients") — givers optimize for the recipient's *immediate* reaction (smile at unwrapping) over durable value, which diverges from what recipients actually end up valuing. [search snippet only, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2733341] — could not verify a distinct "Yang & Urminsky 2018 Foresight of Givers" title separately from this SSRN paper.

**Courtship, costly signaling, and gift-as-love**
- Belk, R.W. & Coon, G.S. (1993), *Journal of Consumer Research*, 20(3), 393–417. "Gift Giving as Agapic Love: An Alternative to the Exchange Paradigm Based on Dating Experiences." Diary-based study of dating couples found gift-giving motives range from transactional to purely symbolic/agapic (unconditional, love-motivated) — an alternative to the reciprocal-exchange model of gifts. [search snippet only, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/20/3/393/1838979]
- Zahavi's handicap principle (1975) and Geoffrey Miller's extension to human courtship signals (humor, art, altruism as "costly" fitness displays) — the theoretical basis that costly/effortful signals are trusted as honest precisely because low-value signalers can't afford to fake them. [search snippet only, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handicap_principle; note the article itself flags this as contested/unconfirmed empirically — cite as theory, not settled fact]
- Mauss, *The Gift* (1925) — not independently re-verified this session, but its "obligation to give/receive/reciprocate" framework is the standard anthropological reference underlying Belk & Coon; [could not verify beyond general knowledge this session].

**Dating-app effort signals (product data, not academic research)**
- Hinge press release: matches where the first message got a reply within 24 hours were 72% more likely to lead to a date; 44% of daters cite low responsiveness as a top dating frustration. [verified by fetch, https://hinge.co/newsroom/your-turn-limits-test]
- Hinge's original 2017 "Your Turn" nudge reduced ghosting by ~25%; the 2024+ "Your Turn Limits" test raised responsiveness ~20% and 48% of users said it helped them focus on quality over quantity. [search snippet only, https://hinge.co/newsroom/your-turn-limits]
- Hinge Roses: a plain Rose converts ~2–3x a standard like; a Rose paired with a specific comment converts ~5x — i.e., stacking an effort signal (the Rose) with a personalized note compounds. [search snippet only, figures vary by source — treat as directional, not exact — https://unil.ink/blog/how-does-hinge-work-2026, https://www.swipestats.io/blog/hinge-statistics]
- Bumble: a low-effort ("one word") opener is flagged and the sender is prompted to write something more thoughtful before sending; internal testing showed mutual-chat-rate gains "more than twice" as large for women as men under the updated policy, and fewer unmatches/expirations. [verified by fetch, https://www.goodmorningamerica.com/living/story/bumbles-women-messaging-135646428] Framing quote: "When a man initiates, he signals that his interest is not accidental and that he is willing to pay a price in time, effort and the risk of rejection." [search snippet only — attribution/source of this exact quote not independently confirmed]
- Tinder Super Like: Tinder's own marketing claims ~3x more matches; one independent 100-vs-100 experiment found 17% vs. 11% match rate (Super Like vs. regular like) — a real but much smaller effect than the marketing figure, and top-decile Super-Like senders actually under-perform the median, suggesting effort signals lose value when overused/cheapened. [search snippet only, could not verify original data source — https://www.swipestats.io/blog/super-like-on-tinder]

**"Reverse choice" / giver-picks-recipient mechanics**
- Buy Nothing Project: rules explicitly give the giver full discretion over who receives an item; community norms favor recipients who explain *why* they want it (not first-come-first-served), and observers note givers respond to visible enthusiasm/thoughtfulness in a request. This is the closest existing real-world analogue to Keepsake's mechanic. [search snippet only, https://docs.buynothingproject.org/fine-print-questions, https://nextcity.org/urbanist-news/are-buy-nothing-groups-really-free]
- "The Bachelor"/"Bachelorette": psychology commentary describes being selected as inflating perceived value and attachment via scarcity and idealization ("being chosen" heightens felt desirability), though academic rigor here is thin (mostly magazine-style psych commentary, not peer-reviewed). Actual relationship success rate across the franchise is only ~11–30%. [search snippet only, https://www.psypost.org/the-bachelor-and-the-bachelorette-provide-evidence-for-evolutionary-theories-of-partner-selection/, https://www.elitedaily.com/entertainment/bachelor-psychological-experiment/1102941]
- Directed kidney donation: donor chooses a specific named recipient; found substantial material on the psychological complexity of donor experience generally, but *not found*: research specifically on how it feels to be chosen as a directed-donation recipient, or a stranger-directed variant analogous to Keepsake. [could not verify this specific angle]

**Loneliness & prosocial-spending data**
- U.S. Surgeon General's Advisory (2023), Murthy: ~50% of US adults report loneliness; effect on mortality risk comparable to smoking; loneliness highest among young adults, not the elderly. [search snippet only, https://www.hhs.gov / summarized via multiple secondary sources — original PDF not independently fetched this session]
- Gallup (2025): daily loneliness among US adults at 20%, a two-year high (down from a pandemic peak of 25%); men aged 15–34 report 25% daily loneliness vs. 18% national average. [search snippet only, https://news.gallup.com/poll/651881/daily-loneliness-afflicts-one-five.aspx, https://news.gallup.com/poll/690788/younger-men-among-loneliest-west.aspx]
- Cigna, *Loneliness in America 2025* (fielded May–June 2024, n>7,500): 57% of Americans classify as lonely; 67% of Gen Z vs. 44% of Boomers. [search snippet only — I attempted to fetch the PDF directly and could not extract readable text this session, so treat these figures as secondary-source-reported, not independently confirmed — https://filecache.mediaroom.com/mr5mr_thecignagroup/183661/2025-loneliness-in-america-report-the-cigna-group.pdf]
- Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008, *Science*) prosocial-spending finding — spending money on others predicts greater happiness than spending on oneself — replicates inconsistently: a larger registered replication (Aknin, Dunn, Proulx, Lok & Norton) did not replicate the original effect size on the primary measure, though a more targeted happiness measure did show an effect in the same direction. **Do not cite this as a clean, settled finding** — the size of the effect is contested in the literature itself. [search snippet only, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9451075/, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32250135/]
- Kumar & Epley / related work on kindness to strangers: consistent finding that givers underestimate gratitude and positive impact of small kindnesses toward strangers, and that this underestimation itself discourages people from being generous more often — directly relevant to Keepsake's "will a stranger even want this" hesitation. [search snippet only, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-026-10240-7, https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fxge0001271]

## Risks/Objections
- No study examines Keepsake's actual mechanic (photo + story + note + giver-picks). Every "reverse choice" analogue (Buy Nothing, Bachelor, directed organ donation) is a loose structural parallel, not a validated causal chain — present as "resonant analogy," not "proven psychology."
- The prosocial-spending literature (Dunn/Aknin/Norton) has a real, published replication problem; using it without the caveat risks a credibility hit if a reader checks.
- The handicap-principle/costly-signaling frame is explicitly contested among the biologists who study it — cite as a theoretical lens, not settled science.
- Dating-app statistics are almost all company-reported PR figures, not independent peer-reviewed research; the Super Like case actually shows heavy users see diminishing/negative returns, a caution against over-claiming "effort always wins."

## Three ways Keepsake could use this
1. **Onboarding copy for givers:** "Research on gift-giving shows people almost always underestimate how much a sentimental object will mean to someone else — read the notes, and you'll see it." Directly leans on Givi & Galak / Kumar & Epley without overclaiming.
2. **The note field as a "costly signal":** frame writing a note (not just tapping a heart) the way Hinge frames a Rose or Bumble frames a real opener — an explicit "this takes 30 seconds of real thought, and that's the point" prompt shown before someone submits their note.
3. **A campaign titled "You get to choose"** built around the Buy Nothing/Bachelor insight that being selected — not just receiving — is what people find meaningful; market the giver's *choosing moment* as the emotional core of the app, not the free object itself.
