# Breakup-Object Rehoming: Evidence for Keepsake's "Ex Objects" Angle

**Verdict:** The angle is genuinely strong on *demand for keeping/discarding* (a solid, cited survey shows 65% of Americans still hold onto ex items, and brands keep building Valentine's stunts around ex-object disposal, proving press appetite) but weak on *proof a marketplace-for-ex-stuff succeeds as a business* — Never Liked It Anyway is the closest precedent and it never grew into a real platform, appears mostly dormant despite claiming to still be online, and no other pure-play "breakup marketplace" was found. The single best piece of evidence is the Neighbor.com/Pollfish survey (Feb 2022, n=1,000): 65% of Americans still own an item from an ex, nearly half for 5+ years, and 78% say they'll get rid of it "someday" — this is a large, latent, self-acknowledged "should declutter but haven't" population, which is exactly the psychological trigger (a name, a deadline, a good home) that a free rehoming feature could convert into listings. Keepsake shouldn't build the whole product around exes, but a seasonal/emotional "give it a good home" framing around breakup objects is well evidenced as press-bait and has a real underlying behavioral base rate; it just needs to be a marketing/content shelf, not the core value proposition, since no direct competitor proves people will transact (even for free) around ex items at scale.

## Risks/Objections Specific to This Angle
- **No surviving proof-of-business-model:** Never Liked It Anyway is the one direct precedent and shows no evidence of sustained traction after its 2011–2012 launch press cycle; the LA Museum of Broken Relationships (the closest "give away your ex's stuff with a story" venue) closed after 18 months. Keepsake could cite the emotion but should not assume the transaction behavior is proven.
- **Sensitivity/negativity of framing:** stories about exes risk skewing toward score-settling, revenge, or oversharing (e.g. "haul" videos mocking an ex) rather than the warm, generous tone Keepsake wants; a giver's "story" field could turn into an ex-shaming venue, which cuts against a wholesome community brand and could scare off recipients who don't want to be someone's emotional dumping ground.
- **Recipient-side stigma:** would a Houston stranger want a stranger's "gift from my ex" candle holder specifically because of that history, or despite it? The angle assumes the story adds appeal; it may instead suppress interest (unlike Museum of Broken Relationships, where the object is display-only, not something you take home and use).
- **Seasonality risk:** virtually all strong evidence (zoo cockroaches, Tinder truck, P.F. Chang's, #ShredYourEx) is Valentine's/anti-Valentine's-timed (Feb), meaning this is a punchy seasonal campaign hook, not sustained daily content — Keepsake would need a plan for the other 11 months.
- **No US/Houston-specific evidence:** the survey (Neighbor.com) is US-national and useful, but every marketplace/press precedent found is either UK/international, LA (defunct), Zagreb, or India (Tinder) — nothing Houston-local was found to ground the launch market.

## 3 Ways Keepsake Could Use This Angle
1. **Seasonal feed shelf:** a "Moving On" or "Letting Go" collection around Valentine's/anti-Valentine's week (mid-to-late Feb) surfacing keepsakes tagged with breakup-adjacent stories, riding the same calendar moment zoos and Tinder already prove gets press.
2. **Campaign copy line:** "Someone kept it in a drawer for years. Someone else would love the story." — directly answers the Neighbor.com finding that most people hold ex-items for 5+ years but say they'll eventually get rid of them; positions Keepsake as the "someday" they keep postponing.
3. **PR hook, not core feature:** pitch Houston press a light stunt around National Breakup Day (Feb 21, per P.F. Chang's precedent) — "Houston's first free rehoming day for breakup keepsakes" — borrowing the proven earned-media formula (zoo cockroaches, shredding services, dumpling giveaways) without building a permanent "ex objects" category on the platform itself.
